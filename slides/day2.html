Queueing

Near capacity, the tail grows much faster than the load

  • Below capacity, a request waits for little more than its own prefill
  • Near capacity, every burst queues, and the 95th percentile shows it first
  • In a self-hosted engine the key-value cache is the hidden limit: when it fills, requests are preempted and computed again
What goes wrong. Sizing on average throughput: the engine is "only 80% busy" while a third of users wait seconds for the first token.

The measurement method (prefill and decode costs, cache arithmetic, curves) is AE-401's; this course uses the curve to decide. If the room has not taken AE-401, the next slide is enough to read one.

The operating point

Operate below the peak: 16 in flight, not 20

The rule

The highest concurrency with goodput within 95% of the peak and first-token p95 at most 80% of the threshold.

Why not 20

Its p95 is 1,295 ms against 1,500: a slightly longer prompt or an Arabic-heavy hour tips it over.

16 in flight gives 2.13 good requests a second per replica, 99% of the peak.

The 80% figure is a course choice for headroom; a team may choose differently, but must choose in writing. Participants defend this rule in the capstone.

The standard you set

A capacity standard has four lines

measure:   goodput curve per replica, real language mix, every engine or model change
operate:   16 in flight per replica (95% of peak goodput, p95 TTFT <= 1,200 ms)
size:      busiest hour of the last 30 days x 1.25, rounded up to whole replicas
review:    monthly, and before any planned sale or campaign

This is what an expert brings to another team: not the curve, the rule that turns the curve into a decision. AE-307 covers capacity from first principles if a participant wants the arithmetic behind replica sizing.

Shedding

Shed early and cheaply, with a message the user understands

  • A request shed at the gateway costs microseconds and returns at once
  • A request that times out in the engine used capacity and still failed
  • A queued P2 request older than 1 s would miss its objective anyway: drop it
  • Tell the user in their language: "Athar is busy; try again in a minute"

Shedding is the first degraded mode (LO3): service kept for the most important traffic. The message matters: a silent failure invites a retry, a clear one invites patience.

Lab 2.4 · retry storm

A 30-second fault becomes an outage that never ends

Client policyPeak offeredRecovered
immediate retries, up to 324 a secondnever
exponential backoff only34 a second63 s after the fault
10% retry budget6.6 a secondwhen the fault ends

6 requests a second offered, 8.5 served at most. Past the knee goodput collapses, so retries keep the service overloaded.

The SRE book describes a per-request limit of three attempts and a per-client retry budget of about 10% of requests, and recommends randomised exponential backoff. Backoff alone spreads the storm; the budget stops it.

Sources: Google SRE book, Handling Overload and Addressing Cascading Failures.

Checkpoint

The fault is gone; the outage is not

A 30-second provider fault ended ten minutes ago. Athar still fails most requests, and the engine shows three times the normal request rate. What is the most likely cause?

Client retries keep the load past the knee, so goodput stays near zero
A new provider fault started

Why. Three times the normal rate right after a fault is the signature of retries. Shed load and enforce a retry budget; the service then recovers.
